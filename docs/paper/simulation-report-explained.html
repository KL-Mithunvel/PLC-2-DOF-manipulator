The 2-DOF robotic arm report, explained simply

The company designed a small robot arm that picks up a 5 kg block with a magnet and swings it 90° to another spot. Before building it, they tested it inside a computer (Ansys 2023 R1) to answer one question: will any part bend too much or break? Short answer from the report: no, it's safe.

The big idea

What the report actually does

  1. Clean up the CAD model. Remove tiny details the computer doesn't need.
  2. Assign materials. Tell the software which parts are steel and which are aluminium.
  3. Mesh it. Chop every part into thousands of tiny pieces (5 mm each) so the math can be done.
  4. Static test. Freeze the arm in two poses and apply gravity + the 5 kg load. Measure bending and stress.
  5. Transient (moving) test. Actually move the arm through a full pick-and-place cycle and measure again.
  6. Conclude. Find the weakest spot and check it still has a good safety margin.
Pages 1–2 · Model description

The 5 bodies

A real arm has dozens of pieces: bolts, bearings, screws, brackets. Simulating each is slow, so they glued groups of parts together into 5 solid "bodies". Each body is treated as one chunk of metal.

Body 1 · steel

Base

The box at the bottom, including the motor housing. It's bolted to the table, so it never moves.

Body 2 · steel

Joint

Joint motor + bearing housing + pin shaft. It spins around the vertical Y-axis, which swings the whole arm left/right.

Body 3 · aluminium

Link-1

The long horizontal arm: two side plates (Arm-1, Arm-2), a cross member joining them, and the pin shaft at the far end.

Body 4 · aluminium

Link-2 + magnet

The short downward arm and the electromagnet that grabs the load. Merged into one body.

Body 5 · steel

5 kg load

A steel cylinder standing in for whatever object the arm lifts. Its own weight is the load.

Why simplify? Bolts, screws and bearings were deleted and small features (holes, fillets) suppressed. They barely change how the arm bends overall, but they make the mesh messy and the solve slow. "Idealized contacts" means they told the software that touching parts are simply stuck together.
Pages 2–3 · Materials

Steel vs aluminium

The heavy, stationary parts use mild steel (AISI 1010). The moving arms use aluminium 6061, which is almost as strong but about one third the weight. Lighter arms mean smaller motors.

Side-by-side, to scale

Mild steel AISI 1010Aluminium 6061
Density
How heavy per volume. Steel 7872 vs aluminium 2713 kg/m³, so aluminium is ~2.9× lighter.
Yield strength
The stress at which the metal starts to permanently bend. Below it, the part springs back. This is the number the safety factor is based on. Steel 250, aluminium 259.2 MPa: nearly equal.
Ultimate strength
The stress at which it actually breaks. Steel 460, aluminium 313.1 MPa.
Young's modulus
Stiffness: how hard it is to stretch or bend. Steel 200 GPa is ~3× stiffer than aluminium 68.9 GPa. That's why the aluminium links bend the most.
Bulk modulus
Resistance to being squeezed from all sides. Steel 166.6, aluminium 58 GPa. Rarely matters for an arm.
Poisson's ratio
When you stretch a bar it gets slightly thinner. This number (0.3 and 0.33) says how much.

Units: 1 MPa = 1 N per mm². So 250 MPa means each square millimetre can carry about 25 kg before yielding.

Page 3 · Meshing

Chopping it into tiny pieces

Computers can't solve one big complicated shape directly. So the software splits each body into many small tetrahedra and cubes ("elements"), about 5 mm wide here. It solves simple equations for each little piece and joins the answers. This method is called Finite Element Analysis (FEA).

Smaller elements = more accurate but slower. 5 mm is a typical compromise for a part this size.
Pages 3–6 · Static structural

Test 1: freeze the arm and load it

"Static" means nothing is moving. The arm is held still in a pose, and the software asks: with gravity pulling down, how much does each part sag, and how hard is the metal working?

Three things are set up:

  • Base fixed: bolted down, zero movement.
  • Earth gravity in −Y: every part feels its own weight.
  • Load: the 5 kg block's own weight hangs on the magnet.

They tested two poses:

Pose A · Figs 4–6

Pick-up position

Arm straight out, just grabbed the block.

Pose B · Figs 7–8

Holding, rotated 90°

Arm swung 90° about the base and the upper arm tilted up, still holding the block. Ansys label: "Up – Rotate 90_Deg".

Three results they measured

Total deformation
How far each point moves from where it started, in mm. Colours in the Ansys plots: blue = barely moved, red = moved most.
Equivalent (von Mises) stress
One number that combines all the pushing, pulling and twisting inside the metal, so you can compare it straight against the yield strength.
Factor of Safety (FOS)
FOS = yield strength ÷ actual stress. FOS 2 means the part could carry twice the stress before permanently bending. Ansys caps the display at 15, so "15" really means "15 or more, very safe".

Deformation: how far each body moves (mm)

Pose A: pick-upPose B: holding at 90°
Biggest value: Link-2 at 0.085 mm. A human hair is about 0.07 mm thick, so the tip sags roughly one hair's width. The base moves 0.0016 mm, invisible.

Stress at pick-up (MPa), against yield

The bars are drawn on a 0–260 MPa scale (the yield line is the right edge). All stresses are tiny, under 9 MPa, so the bars barely show. That's the point: the metal is barely working. The report does not show stress plots for Pose B, only FOS.

Minimum Factor of Safety per body

Pose A: pick-upPose B: holding at 90°FOS = 1 (yield)
The joint at Pose B has the lowest FOS: 4.631. This is the weakest spot in the whole report and becomes the headline number in the conclusion ("4.6").
Why the joint? The joint holds up the whole arm plus the load, like your shoulder holding a bag at arm's length. The long lever creates a large twisting moment right at the joint. Tilting the arm up in Pose B changes how that load passes through the joint, which raises its stress.
Pages 6–9 · Transient structural

Test 2: move the arm through a full cycle

"Transient" means things change with time. Instead of frozen poses, they made the motors actually turn and watched stress and bending over a 120-second pick-and-place cycle. Speeding up or slowing down adds extra forces (inertia), and this test catches those.

Each motor was given an angle-vs-time recipe (the graphs on page 7). Press play or drag the slider to see what those recipes do.

Recreated from the three graphs on page 7. Ansys plots the joint as 0 to −90° (the minus is just direction); shown here as 0–90°. Motion is ramped linearly between points.

In plain words, the cycle is:

  1. 0–30 s: grab the block at the origin and swing 90° to the target.
  2. 30–60 s: tilt the arms up and back down to place the block.
  3. 60–90 s: swing back 90° to the origin.
  4. 90–120 s: tilt up and down again to pick/drop the block at the origin.
"Smooth ramping" means the angle changes gradually, not instantly. An instant jump would mean infinite acceleration and fake, huge forces. Over 120 s this motion is very slow, so the dynamic forces are small and results end up close to the static ones.

Page 9 · Conclusion

What they concluded

4.6

The lowest safety factor anywhere, found at the joint while holding the 5 kg load after a 90° turn. The joint could take about 4.6× more stress before permanently bending.

From that, the report says:

  • The joint is strong enough for the moving loads.
  • Steel and aluminium are good material choices.
  • The arm is stiff and strong enough overall.
  • The design is fit for 2-DOF pick-and-place.
Reading it critically

Things worth questioning

The overall conclusion looks reasonable, but a careful reader (or examiner) might ask about these.

"Well above the acceptable limit" — which limit?The report never states the target FOS. Common machine-design targets are around 1.5–3, so 4.6 likely passes, but the requirement should be written down.
Joint FOS at pick-up doesn't match its stressJoint max stress is 8.8 MPa. 250 ÷ 8.8 ≈ 28, which should display as 15, yet the FOS plot shows 9.79. The FOS plots were time-stamped a few minutes before the stress plots, so they may come from a different solve.
"2-DOF" but three things rotateThe base swing, Link-1 and Link-2 all have their own motion. That's three rotations. Either one is not independently driven, or the arm is really 3-DOF.
Link-2 angle: 15° or 10°?The text says 0–15°, but the page 7 graph peaks at 10°.
Some legends don't match their headersFig 9 header says max 0.0058 mm, legend tops out at 0.0008. Fig 12 says 0.0010 vs legend 0.00069. Probably custom legend ranges, but confusing.
No stress or FOS over time for the moving testOnly end-of-run plots (t = 120 s) are shown. The worst moment could be mid-cycle, during a swing.
Aluminium bulk modulusFrom E and Poisson's ratio, K = 68.9 ÷ (3 × 0.34) ≈ 67.5 GPa, not 58. Minor, and it doesn't affect these results.
Bolts and bearings removedFine for overall bending, but in reality bolts and bearings are often where joints fail. A separate bolt/bearing check would make the "joint is safe" claim stronger.
Cheat sheet

Glossary

DOF
Degrees of freedom: how many independent ways the arm can move.
CAD clean-up
Simplifying the 3D model before simulation.
FEA
Finite Element Analysis: solving physics by splitting a part into small elements.
Mesh / element
The grid of small pieces, and each piece.
Static analysis
Loads applied, nothing moving.
Transient analysis
Loads and motion that change over time; includes inertia.
Fixed support
A boundary that can't move at all.
Deformation
How far a point moves under load (mm).
von Mises stress
One combined stress number to compare with yield strength.
Yield strength
Stress at which permanent bending starts.
Factor of Safety
Yield strength ÷ actual stress. Bigger is safer.
